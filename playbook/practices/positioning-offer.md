# Positioning, buying roles and offers

A positioning statement helps a buyer judge "is this for me". Start by filling in: we help [type of team], at [which moment], get [which job] done; compared with [current approach], we offer [a difference we can prove].

This is an internal working card, not homepage copy. Replace every abstract word with the customer's actual task.

## Start from the alternative

Competitors are not only similar software. Customers may use search, spreadsheets, an intern, an agency, or nothing at all. Ask what they lose in time, missed opportunities and switching cost, then decide what to emphasize. For the method behind this, see [April Dunford's writing on positioning](https://www.aprildunford.com/category/positioning); the table below is an independent working template.

## Separate the buying roles

| Role | Wants to know | Material to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| User | Is it easier than today, is the output usable | Task demo, sample, import and export notes |
| Champion | How to explain it internally and drive the evaluation | One-page proposal, pilot goals, results log |
| Budget owner | Why buy now, what it costs and returns | Bounded quote, cost comparison, success criteria |
| Security or procurement | Are data, liability, payment and contract acceptable | Accurate data handling notes, terms, procurement documents |

One person can hold several roles. Do not guess budget authority from a job title; ask about the process.

## Six slots of an offer

1. Who buys it and for which job.
2. The final deliverable, not a feature count.
3. Input requirements and exclusions.
4. Delivery time and how acceptance is judged.
5. Price, billing unit and overage handling.
6. Remedy and support if it fails.

Example: an account research package for one event can specify the company filters, sources, update date and review method. Without verified attendance, do not promise a "complete, verified attendee list"; providing data does not guarantee meetings or deals.

## Starting on price

Test which unit buyers understand value in: seats, usage, projects, events or outcomes. List direct costs, manual support and the cost of failed tasks, and check that heavy usage does not lose money.

Keep a few easy-to-explain options early on. Low-priced self-serve and enterprise service can coexist with separate delivery promises. Trade discounts for a clear commitment or scope; do not permanently change price because the first buyer negotiated.

When you hear "too expensive", ask what they compared it with, which budget cycle, and which doubt about value. Low sales can be a price problem, or demand, trust, procurement or experience. Work through it item by item with the [paid pilot template](../templates/paid-pilot.md).
